**Short answer:** In California, a late fee in a residential lease counts as liquidated damages. Under Civil Code §1671(d), applied to leases by §1951.5, those fees are void unless actual damages from late rent would be impracticable or extremely difficult to fix. In *Orozco v. Casimiro* (2004), a $50 late fee was held void because the landlord never proved that.

## Why a late fee is "liquidated damages" in California

A late fee is an amount you and your landlord agreed on ahead of time to cover the landlord's loss when rent comes in late. California law calls that kind of pre-set amount **liquidated damages**.

[Civil Code §1951.5](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=CIV&sectionNum=1951.5.) says that §1671, the liquidated damages statute, "applies to a lease of real property."

## What Civil Code §1671(d) says

[Section 1671](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=1671.&lawCode=CIV) has two sets of rules:

- **Most contracts (§1671(b)):** a liquidated damages clause is valid unless the person challenging it shows it was unreasonable.
- **Dwelling leases (§1671(c)(2) and (d)):** the clause "is void," with one exception. It can stand when "from the nature of the case, it would be impracticable or extremely difficult to fix the actual damage."

For a home you rent and live in, the default is reversed. The fee starts out void, and the exception has to apply.

## What *Orozco v. Casimiro* decided

In [*Orozco v. Casimiro*](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2259549/orozco-v-casimiro/) (2004) 121 Cal.App.4th Supp. 7, a Los Angeles tenant's lease set rent due on the 10th, with a "$50.00 3 days grace" late charge. The landlord served a notice over an unpaid $50 late fee.

The Appellate Division of the Los Angeles County Superior Court held:

- A residential late-fee clause is "normally void" under §1671(d) unless the exception applies.
- The landlord "neither pleaded nor proved" that his damages were impracticable or extremely difficult to fix, so the $50 late fee was "void and unenforceable."
- The court also **rejected** the tenant's argument that late fees are *always* void. A landlord's losses can include interest plus administrative costs reasonably related to collecting and accounting for late payments.

**Keep in mind:** *Orozco* came from an appellate division of a superior court. Other California courts can find it persuasive, but it does not bind every court in the state. Outcomes depend on the facts and the evidence.

## Late fees charged inside your grace period

Before you get to §1671, there's a simpler question: **was the fee even triggered under your own lease?**

Many leases give a grace period, like the three days in *Orozco*. If your ledger shows a late fee posted on a day you were still inside that window, or after a payment that cleared on time, the charge may not match your lease terms at all.

Check:

1. **Due date and grace period** in your lease. Write down the exact day a late fee can start.
2. **The date your payment was made or posted.** Use bank records, portal receipts, or money-order stubs.
3. **The date the late fee appears** on the ledger.
4. **Repeats:** whether the same late fee was charged twice for one month, or charged again the next month on an unpaid late fee.

## How to raise it with your landlord

- Ask for your **full tenant ledger** in writing.
- Make a short table: date, charge, amount, and the lease section it should match (or "not in lease").
- Send a polite written request to correct or reverse the charge, attach your proof, and keep copies.
- For advice about your own situation, contact a local tenant organization, legal aid office, or a licensed attorney.
